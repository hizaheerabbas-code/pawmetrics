How Much Should I Feed My Dog? Complete Guide 2026

📅 Updated May 2026 ⏱️ 12 min read 🐕 All breeds covered

Feeding your dog the right amount is one of the most important things you can do for their health and longevity. Too much food causes obesity — which affects over 50% of dogs in the USA and UK. Too little causes malnutrition. This complete guide tells you exactly how much to feed your dog based on their weight, age, breed size and activity level.

Quick Answer — How Much to Feed Your Dog

The amount of food your dog needs depends on four key factors: body weight, age, activity level and the type of food you are feeding. There is no single answer that works for every dog, but here is a reliable starting point:

General Rule: Most adult dogs need approximately 2% to 3% of their ideal body weight in dry food per day. A 10kg dog needs around 200–300g of dry kibble daily. A 30kg dog needs around 400–500g daily.

However, this general rule is just a starting point. Puppies need significantly more, senior dogs need less, and very active dogs like working breeds or sport dogs can need up to 40% more calories than a sedentary dog of the same size.

Feeding Guide by Age — Puppy, Adult and Senior

A dog's nutritional needs change dramatically across their lifetime. What is right for a 3-month-old puppy is completely wrong for a 9-year-old senior dog. Here is what you need to know at each life stage.

Feeding a Puppy (Under 12 Months)

Puppies have tiny stomachs but enormous energy needs. They need roughly twice the calories per kilogram of body weight compared to an adult dog. This supports their rapid bone development, muscle growth and immune system maturation.

  • Puppies under 3 months: feed 4 times per day
  • Puppies 3–6 months: feed 3 times per day
  • Puppies 6–12 months: feed 2–3 times per day
  • Always use puppy-specific food — adult food does not have the right calcium to phosphorus ratio for healthy bone development
  • Large breed puppies need specially formulated large breed puppy food to prevent too-rapid growth, which can cause joint problems

Puppy tip: Never free-feed a puppy. Scheduled meal times are essential for house training, monitoring food intake and preventing obesity. Weigh your puppy every week and adjust portions as they grow.

Feeding an Adult Dog (1–7 Years)

Once your dog reaches adulthood — around 12 months for small breeds, 18–24 months for large breeds — you can switch to adult formula food. Adult dogs do best on two meals per day at consistent times.

The amount of food an adult dog needs depends primarily on their weight and activity level. A working Labrador running 5 miles a day needs dramatically more food than a Labrador who mostly sleeps on the sofa. Use our calculator to get an accurate figure based on your specific dog's lifestyle.

Feeding a Senior Dog (7+ Years)

Senior dogs have slower metabolisms and are typically less active than younger adults. Most senior dogs need about 20% fewer calories than a middle-aged adult of the same weight. Feeding the same amount as you did when they were younger is one of the leading causes of obesity in older dogs.

  • Switch to senior formula food at age 7 for large breeds, age 8–9 for small breeds
  • Senior food has fewer calories but more joint-supporting nutrients like glucosamine
  • Increase vet check-ups to twice per year to monitor weight and health
  • Some senior dogs develop dental issues — softer food or wet food can help

Dry Food vs Wet Food — How Much Should I Feed?

The type of food you feed your dog makes a huge difference to the portion size. Dry kibble and wet food have very different calorie densities because of their water content.

Dry Kibble

Dry kibble typically contains around 8–10% moisture and provides approximately 300–400 calories per 100g. It is the most common type of dog food in the USA and UK and is generally the most cost-effective option. Portion sizes are smaller by weight, making it easier to measure accurately.

Wet / Canned Food

Wet food contains 70–80% moisture and typically provides only 80–100 calories per 100g. This means you need to feed roughly 3–4 times as much by weight to provide the same calories as dry kibble. Wet food is excellent for keeping dogs hydrated and is often more palatable for fussy eaters or dogs with dental issues.

Raw Food Diet (BARF)

A raw food diet (Biologically Appropriate Raw Food) typically provides around 130–180 calories per 100g. Most raw feeding guidelines recommend feeding 2–3% of your dog's body weight per day in raw food. A 20kg dog on a raw diet would therefore need approximately 400–600g of raw food daily.

Mixing tip: Many owners feed a combination of dry and wet food. If doing this, reduce each portion proportionally. For example, if your dog needs 300g of dry food, you might give 150g dry food and the wet food equivalent of 150g dry (approximately 450–500g wet).

How Many Times a Day Should I Feed My Dog?

Meal frequency is just as important as meal size. Here is the simple answer for most dogs:

  • Puppies under 4 months: 4 meals per day
  • Puppies 4–6 months: 3 meals per day
  • Dogs 6 months to adult: 2–3 meals per day
  • Adult dogs (1 year+): 2 meals per day
  • Senior dogs (7 year+): 2 meals per day

Why Twice a Day is Best for Adult Dogs

Feeding twice a day — morning and evening — helps maintain stable blood sugar levels, reduces the risk of bloat (especially in large deep-chested breeds), makes house training easier, and allows you to monitor appetite changes that could indicate illness.

Should I Feed My Dog Once a Day?

While some owners feed their dogs once a day, this is generally not recommended by vets. A single large meal can cause gastric discomfort and increases the risk of bloat in large and giant breeds. It also means your dog goes up to 24 hours without food, which is not ideal for their energy levels and digestion.

What About Free Feeding?

Free feeding — leaving food out all day for your dog to graze — is strongly discouraged for most dogs. It almost always leads to overeating and obesity, removes appetite as a health monitoring tool, and is impractical with wet food which spoils quickly. The only situation where free feeding may be appropriate is for very young puppies still developing.

Is My Dog Overweight? How to Tell

Over 50% of dogs in the USA and UK are overweight or obese according to veterinary surveys. The problem is that many owners simply cannot tell their dog is carrying too much weight — especially with fluffy or thick-coated breeds.

The Rib Test

The most reliable at-home check is the rib test. Run your hands along the sides of your dog's chest:

  • Ideal weight: You can feel the ribs easily with slight pressure — like feeling your knuckles through a thin glove
  • Underweight: Ribs are clearly visible and prominent without any touching
  • Overweight: You have to press firmly to feel ribs through a thick layer of fat
  • Obese: Ribs cannot be felt at all

Body Condition Score (BCS)

Veterinarians use a Body Condition Score (BCS) on a scale of 1 to 9 to assess dog weight. A score of 4–5 is ideal. A score of 6–7 is overweight. A score of 8–9 is obese. Ask your vet to assess your dog's BCS at their next check-up.

Warning signs of obesity in dogs: Difficulty breathing after light exercise, inability to feel ribs, visible fat deposits around the neck and base of the tail, reluctance to exercise, and a waist that is wider than the chest when viewed from above.

How Activity Level Changes Your Dog's Food Needs

Activity level is one of the most underestimated factors in dog feeding. Two dogs of exactly the same weight and age can have calorie needs that differ by 40% or more depending on how active they are.

This is why generic feeding guides on food packaging — which are always written for average activity levels — so often lead to overfeeding in less active dogs.

Dog Feeding Guide by Breed Size

Small Breeds (Under 10kg) — Chihuahua, Pug, Shih Tzu, Yorkie

Small breed dogs have faster metabolisms than large breeds and burn more calories per kilogram of body weight. They need calorie-dense food in small portions. Small breed specific formulas are designed with smaller kibble pieces and higher calorie density to meet their needs without overloading their tiny digestive systems.

Feed small breeds 2–3 meals per day throughout adulthood. Their blood sugar can drop more quickly than large breeds, making regular meals important.

Medium Breeds (10–25kg) — Beagle, Cocker Spaniel, Boxer, Border Collie

Medium breeds are the most straightforward to feed. Standard adult formulas work well. Two meals per day is ideal. The key is monitoring weight regularly — medium breeds like Labradors and Beagles are particularly prone to obesity if owners are not careful with portions.

Large Breeds (25–45kg) — Labrador, German Shepherd, Golden Retriever

Large breeds have lower metabolisms per kilogram than small breeds and need less food relative to their body weight. However, because of their absolute size, the total daily food amount is still significant. Large breeds are at higher risk of bloat (gastric dilatation) — so feed two meals per day rather than one, avoid exercise immediately after eating, and use a slow feeder bowl if your dog eats very fast.

Giant Breeds (45kg+) — Great Dane, Mastiff, Saint Bernard

Giant breeds have special nutritional needs throughout their lives. Giant breed puppies must eat giant breed puppy food to control their growth rate — too-rapid growth causes devastating joint problems. Adult giant breeds should be fed twice daily and monitored carefully for weight. Their shorter lifespans mean health issues tend to appear earlier, making regular vet monitoring essential.

7 Common Dog Feeding Mistakes to Avoid

1. Eyeballing Portions Instead of Weighing

Studies show that owners who estimate portions by eye consistently overfeed by 10–30%. Use a kitchen scale for the first few weeks until you have an accurate feel for the right amount. Even a small daily excess of 50 calories per day can cause 2kg of weight gain in a year.

2. Not Accounting for Treats

Treats are calories too. If your dog gets significant treats throughout the day, reduce their main meal size accordingly. Treats should make up no more than 10% of total daily calorie intake. Use the calories on the treat packet to track this.

3. Feeding Table Scraps

Table scraps are often high in fat, salt and calories. Many human foods — including onions, grapes, chocolate, avocado and xylitol (artificial sweetener) — are toxic to dogs. Avoid table scraps entirely.

4. Free Feeding Dry Kibble

Leaving a bowl of dry food out all day almost always leads to overeating. Scheduled meal times give you control over exactly how much your dog eats and allows you to spot any changes in appetite that could signal illness.

5. Not Adjusting Food as Your Dog Ages

Many owners continue feeding the same amount throughout their dog's life. A 7-year-old dog needs significantly less food than the same dog at age 3. Failing to reduce food intake as your dog ages is a leading cause of senior dog obesity.

6. Switching Foods Too Quickly

Changing your dog's food abruptly causes digestive upset — loose stools, vomiting and discomfort. Always transition over 7–10 days by gradually increasing the proportion of new food while decreasing the old food.

7. Ignoring the Calorie Content on the Food Label

Different dog foods have very different calorie densities. A premium food may have 380 kcal per 100g while a budget brand has 300 kcal per 100g. If you switch foods without recalculating portions, your dog may suddenly be significantly over or underfed.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should I feed my dog per day? +
Most adult dogs need between 2% and 3% of their body weight in dry food per day. A 10kg dog needs approximately 200–300g of dry kibble daily, split into two meals. Use our free Dog Food Calculator above for a precise amount based on your dog's specific details.
How many cups of food should I feed my dog? +
Cup measurements vary by food density, but as a general guide: small dogs (under 10kg) need ½–1 cup daily; medium dogs (10–25kg) need 1½–2½ cups daily; large dogs (25–40kg) need 3–4 cups daily. Our calculator gives you the exact cup amount for your dog's specific food type.
Can I leave dog food out all day? +
Free feeding (leaving food out all day) is not recommended for most dogs as it almost always leads to overeating and obesity. It also makes wet food unsafe as it spoils quickly. Scheduled meal times twice per day are best for adult dogs.
How do I know if I am feeding my dog enough? +
You should be able to feel your dog's ribs easily but not see them. If ribs are visible your dog is underweight. If you cannot feel ribs at all your dog is overweight. Weigh your dog every 2–4 weeks and ask your vet to assess their Body Condition Score at annual check-ups.
Should I feed my dog wet food or dry food? +
Both dry and wet food are nutritionally complete options. Dry food is more calorie-dense and cost-effective. Wet food provides more hydration and is often preferred by fussy dogs or those with dental issues. Many owners feed a combination of both. The most important factor is choosing a complete and balanced formula appropriate for your dog's age and size.
How much should I feed my dog after neutering? +
Neutered dogs have lower energy needs — typically 20–30% less than intact dogs of the same size and age. Reduce your dog's daily food amount by around 20–25% after neutering and monitor their weight carefully over the following weeks. Many vets recommend switching to a neutered-specific formula which is lower in calories.
